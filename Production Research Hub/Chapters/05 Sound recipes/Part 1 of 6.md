# Anti-Recipes — Hip-Hop (what NOT to do)

Five moves that make rap productions sound amateur, and why.

## Steps
1. Don't let the kick and 808 fight. If both have energy at 60 Hz, you get mud, not power. Either the kick is a click that triggers the 808's attention (trap) or they're EQ-carved into separate lanes (boom-bap). Pick one relationship per song.
2. Don't drown a rap vocal in reverb. Reverb pushes voices back; rap lives in front. If it feels dry and lonely, add a short delay throw or a 0.3 s room at whisper level — not a hall.
3. Don't leave the 808 un-tuned. An 808 is a bass instrument playing notes. If those notes aren't in the song key, nothing above them can fix it. Tune first, then mix.
4. Don't stack five hi-hat loops for texture. Frequency pileup at 8–12 kHz reads as harsh noise. One programmed hat pattern with velocity variation beats five loops every time.
5. Don't master to match a Spotify rip's loudness by limiter alone. Slamming the Maximizer until the meter matches destroys the transients that make drums knock. If you can't reach the loudness cleanly, the mix (usually the low end) isn't ready for the master.

## Why this works
Hip-hop's power is minimalism executed precisely — errors here are almost always "too much" rather than "too little."

## Technique you just learned
Subtraction is the most-used tool in professional rap mixes.

# Anti-Recipes — Rock (what NOT to do)

Five moves that make rock productions sound amateur, and why.

## Steps
1. Don't scoop guitar mids. The bedroom-metal V (bass up, mids gone, treble up) sounds huge solo and vanishes in the mix — mids are where guitars live against vocals and cymbals. Cut 400 Hz mud, keep 1–3 kHz.
2. Don't reverb everything. One shared RV7000 send on drums-plus-vocal puts the band in one room. Six different reverbs puts them in six rooms. Amateur mixes drown; pro rock mixes are drier than you think.
3. Don't double the same take by copying and nudging. A copied-and-delayed track is a chorus effect, not a double. Real doubles (played twice) create width from micro-variation. No shortcut sounds the same.
4. Don't compress the room out of drums. Slamming every drum channel kills the room sound that makes drums feel live. Compress the bus, gently, and leave elements breathing.
5. Don't mix guitars louder than the vocal for more energy. Energy comes from arrangement (what drops out before the chorus) and drum performance, not from guitar volume. If the chorus doesn't lift with guitars at reasonable level, the arrangement is the problem.

## Why this works
Every one of these errors comes from judging tracks solo instead of in the mix.

## Technique you just learned
Solo is a diagnostic tool, not a mixing position.

# Feels: Aggressive

**Accuracy: B** — the harmonic-minor "villain" progression, the 7#9 "Hendrix chord," and
zero-swing "machine groove" are all documented (music theory + production writeups);
combined here into one Reason build.

## The Chain
SubTractor (harmonic minor chords, driven hard) → Kong (tight, hard-clipped kit, zero swing) → Scream 4 (heavy distortion on drums and chords) → MClass Equalizer (aggressive mid push) → MClass Compressor (fast, pumping)

## Steps
1. Set the tempo to 148 BPM — fast, high arousal, well above the "neutral" comfort window.
2. On the Launchkey, start from A, but raise the 7th note (G) up one key to G# — that's harmonic minor, the "dramatic, Arabian/old-world, villain boss music" scale. That one raised note, and the awkward 3-semitone gap it creates right before the octave, is what gives harmonic minor its snarl.
3. Play the progression i–VII–VI–V: A minor, G major, F major, then E7#9 — the "Hendrix chord" (E-G#-B-D-G). It stacks a major 3rd (G#) AND a minor 3rd (G) against the same E root — those two 3rds fighting each other in one chord is the sound of funk aggression and snarl.
4. Play everything staccato and loud — short, hard, detached hits (lift fingers fast, push velocity to 110+). Both staccato articulation and loud dynamics independently push toward anger/aggression rather than calm.
5. Drum feel: this is a RIGID-GRID recipe — set swing to 0%, fixed velocities, dead-on-grid quantize. Zero swing plus rigid velocities reads as cold and mechanical, which is exactly the "machine/menace groove" this feeling wants — the opposite approach from a soulful or nostalgic swing recipe.
6. Drive the chords AND the drum bus hard through Scream 4, Damage type Distortion, amount 50–65 — harsh, roughened timbre is documented to transfer the same emotional read as a distressed human scream, even on instruments.
7. Layer a second detuned copy of the E7#9 chord stab, a few cents apart, both centered (not widened) — the resulting fast beating (roughness in roughly the 30–80 Hz modulation range) is a threat/aggression cue on its own, separate from any sub-bass you might add underneath.
8. On MClass Equalizer, push the low-mids and upper-mids (around 800 Hz–3 kHz) rather than scooping them — an aggressive, present midrange is what makes distortion read as "in your face" instead of muddy.
9. Compress hard and fast on the drum bus (MClass Compressor, fast attack, high ratio) so the drums pump and slam rather than breathe — a tighter, more controlled dynamic range reads as tension held on a leash, ready to snap.

## Why this works
Research found mode moves feeling more than any other single cue, and harmonic minor's
raised 7th sits it at the "dramatic/villain" end of the minor spectrum before anything else
happens. Tempo (fast), register, dynamics (loud), and articulation (staccato) all stack in
the same aggressive direction here, and timbre does unusually heavy lifting: harsh,
distorted, roughened sound is documented to trigger the same defensive/threat response as a
human scream, which is why the distortion in step 6 and the beating stack in step 7 matter
as much as the chord choice. The 7#9 chord itself works because it contains a genuine
harmonic clash (major and minor 3rd at once) — the tension never needs a rhythm section to
exist, it's baked into the chord.

## Order of operations
Harmonic-minor progression and the 7#9 snarl chord first (the aggression starts in the
harmony) → rigid zero-swing drums locked in → distortion and roughness stacking applied
last, as the finishing "teeth" on top of an already-tense chord.

## Reason-specific trick
Automate Scream 4's Damage amount to spike higher exactly on the E7#9 chord hit, then drop
back slightly elsewhere — a chord-synced distortion swell makes that one chord feel like it's
biting harder than the rest. Save the rack as "Aggressive Harmonic Minor Rack.cmb."

## RE upgrade path
A dedicated multiband distortion/saturation RE will let you drive the mids harder while
keeping the low end from folding in on itself — useful once Scream 4 starts feeling too
one-dimensional at high Damage settings.

## Technique you just learned
The 7#9 chord: stack a major 3rd and a minor 3rd on the same root at once — the clash between
"happy" and "sad" living in one chord is the actual sound of a snarl, no distortion required
to feel it (though distortion helps).

## Reference
Purple Haze / 7#9 "Hendrix chord" explained: https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=hendrix+chord+7sharp9+purple+haze+explained

# Feels: Dreamy

**Accuracy: B** — Lydian mode's association with "floating, weightless" scoring (E.T.,
Superman flight, Dune) is documented across film-theory writeups; the detune/chorus and long
reverb chain is standard dream-pop/lo-fi production practice, combined here for Reason.

## The Chain
Malström (two graintable oscillators, detuned) → NN-XT (maj7 pad, doubled) → The Echo (slow, subtle) → RV7000 (huge hall, long tail) → MClass Equalizer (gentle lowpass for haze)

## Steps
1. Set the tempo to 68 BPM — slow enough that each chord has room to just sit there.
2. On the Launchkey, play only white keys starting from F — that's Lydian mode. Unlike every other white-key mode, Lydian's 4th note (B) is a half-step higher than "normal" major, and that one raised note is what removes the feeling of gravity pulling you home.
3. Play a two-chord loop: F major 7 (F-A-C-E), then G major 7 (G-B-D-F#... actually stay on all-white-key G major: G-B-D, add F on top for the maj7 color) — hold each for a full 4 bars. Slow harmonic rhythm (holding chords a long time) is itself a "vastness" cue — the floating feeling comes from time, not motion.
4. On Malström, load a soft pad or graintable patch and detune Oscillator B by +10 cents against Oscillator A — keep both mono, no chorus device needed yet. The slow beating between the two blurs exactly where the pitch "is," which is the dreaminess.
5. Double that same pad on NN-XT one octave up, very quiet underneath — this thickens the haze without adding a new note.
6. Drum feel: keep it sparse — soft brushed hats only, swing around 53%, no snare or a barely-there rimshot. Too much rhythmic information kills the floating quality.
7. Send everything through RV7000 on a huge hall setting: 5–6 second decay, 30 ms predelay. Long reverb is the single biggest "where you are tells you how to feel" cue — this is what makes it feel vast.
8. Roll the MClass Equalizer's high end down gently (a soft lowpass starting around 6–8 kHz) on the final bounce — this "underwater," slightly-submerged quality reads as memory and distance, not lo-fi grit.
9. Add a tiny amount of The Echo (low mix, long slow feedback) just on the pad — enough to smear the edges, not enough to be heard as a distinct echo.

## Why this works
Research found mode moves feeling more than any other single cue, and Lydian's raised 4th
specifically removes the expectation of resolution that major and minor both rely on — so
the chord just hangs there instead of pulling toward home. Tempo (slow) and timbre (soft,
detuned, rolled-off highs) do the next-most work; register and dynamics barely matter here,
which is why this recipe stays quiet and centered rather than reaching high or low.

## Order of operations
Lydian chords and slow harmonic rhythm first (the floating feeling starts in the harmony)
→ detune/double the pad → reverb and lowpass haze applied last, as the "distance" polish.

## Reason-specific trick
Malström's graintable oscillators detune more naturally than SubTractor's — that's why the
pad lives there. Save this whole rack as "Dreamy Lydian Float.cmb" for instant recall.

## RE upgrade path
A dedicated shimmer-reverb RE (pitch-shifted reverb tails, an octave up) will push this even
further into "floating" territory than RV7000 alone.

## Technique you just learned
The Lydian #4: raise the 4th note of a major scale by one half-step and the chord stops
wanting to resolve — it just floats, which is the whole mechanism behind "movie magic" wonder.

## Reference
Lydian mode and the "Hollywood floating" sound: https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=lydian+mode+hollywood+floating+sound+explained

# Feels: Eerie

**Accuracy: B** — the whole-tone "no home note" description, microtonal detuning as a
"wrongness" cue, and the layered-Shepard-tone endless-riser trick (per the Dunkirk case
study) are all documented; combined here into one Reason build.

## The Chain
SubTractor (parallel augmented triads, whole-tone) → Malström (two voices detuned ±30–50 cents, mono) → NN-XT (reversed, pitched vocal chop) → The Echo (long, unsynced feedback) → MClass Equalizer (thin, no warm low-mids)

## Steps
1. Set the tempo to 76 BPM, and don't quantize anything tightly — let the pacing feel a little unmoored.
2. This mode needs black keys — there's no all-white-key trick here. Play the whole-tone scale starting on C: C, D, E, F#, G#, A#, back to C. Every single gap is the same size (two keys/a whole step) — that sameness is exactly why your ear can't find a "home" note. Every other scale in this book has an uneven pattern that tells your ear where it is; this one doesn't.
3. Play parallel augmented triads moving up by whole steps: C augmented (C-E-G#), then D augmented (D-F#-A#) — notice these are actually the same three notes, just starting from a different one. There's no "chord progression" in the normal sense — just the same shape sliding.
4. On Malström, take a pad or lead patch and detune one oscillator −30 to −50 cents against a dry copy, both panned mono (no chorus, no stereo widening) — the slow, uneven beating this produces reads as "alien and wrong" in a way normal dissonance doesn't.
5. Build a Shepard-tone-style endless riser: layer the same note in SubTractor at three different octaves, and slowly automate volume so the low one fades in as the high one fades out (and vice versa) over 8–16 bars — this creates the illusion of a pitch that rises forever without ever arriving, the exact trick behind Dunkirk's dread.
6. Take a vocal chop (any word or "ah" sample) into NN-XT, reverse it, and shift the pitch down 3–5 semitones — reversed, lowered vocal fragments read as "haunted" because they carry human vocal grain without any recognizable meaning.
7. Drum feel: keep it sparse and irregular — if you use drums at all, scatter a few distant, filtered hits with no consistent pulse, zero swing, and long gaps. A missing or unstable pulse is itself unsettling.
8. Send everything through The Echo with feedback around 50%, unsynced to tempo (free-running time) — let the repeats drift slightly out of alignment with the beat over time.
9. On MClass Equalizer, thin out the low-mids (200–500 Hz) rather than the highs — this keeps the sound feeling hollow and distant instead of warm, which is what "wrong" needs.

## Why this works
Research found mode moves feeling more than any other single cue, and the whole-tone scale
is the extreme case — because it has no uneven step pattern, it gives the ear zero sense of
"home," which is precisely the mechanism behind its cartoon "entering a dream/danger
sequence" association. Timbre does unusually heavy lifting in this recipe too: microtonal
detuning and reversed vocal fragments are documented "wrongness" cues that go beyond normal
brightness/darkness. The layered Shepard-tone riser exploits expectation directly — an
illusion of endless rising with no arrival point, which is what makes dread feel unresolvable
rather than building toward a release.

## Order of operations
Whole-tone chord shape first (establish "no home") → detuned pad for wrongness → Shepard-tone
riser layered in → reversed vocal fragment and irregular drum scatter added last, as the
finishing "haunted" texture.

## Reason-specific trick
Automate the three SubTractor octave layers' volumes with a slow LFO synced to a very long
rate (16+ bars) rather than a straight fade — an LFO-driven crossfade sounds less
mechanically obvious than a manual automation ramp. Save the rack as "Eerie Whole-Tone
Shepard.cmb."

## RE upgrade path
A dedicated granular or pitch-shifting RE will build a smoother, more convincing endless
Shepard-tone riser than manually crossfading three SubTractor layers.

## Technique you just learned
The whole-tone scale: make every step in the scale the same size, and the ear loses its
sense of "home" entirely — no note feels more resolved than any other, which is the purest
version of "something is off" available in music theory.

## Reference
Whole-tone scale and dream-sequence sound explained: https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=whole+tone+scale+dream+sequence+eerie+sound+explained

# Feels: Euphoric

**Accuracy: B** — the additive-build/drop-out arrangement trick and the felt-sub-bass groove
research are both documented (EDM production writeups + groove psychology studies);
combined here into one Reason build.

## The Chain
SubTractor (bright, staccato lead + felt sub) → Kong (medium-syncopation groove kit) → The Echo (noise riser into the drop) → MClass Maximizer (loud, arena-ready) → MClass Stereo Imager (wide)

## Steps
1. Set the tempo to 128 BPM — high arousal range, well past the "calm/neutral" 85–120 BPM comfort window.
2. On the Launchkey, play only white keys starting from C — Ionian (major), the happy home mode this whole feeling is built on.
3. Play the progression I–V–vi–IV: C major, G major, A minor, F major — but voice it HIGH (up around C5–C6) and BRIGHT, and play it staccato (short, detached notes, lift your fingers fast) rather than held — high register plus staccato both independently push arousal and joy up.
4. Build additively: start the loop with just a kick and the chord pad, then add one new element every 4–8 bars (hats, then bass, then a lead line) — momentum from accumulating layers is the engine of a build.
5. One bar before your hook, cut almost everything out — kill the kick, the bass, most of the pad — leaving maybe one thin element. A moment of near-nothing right before impact makes the return hit far harder than another riser would.
6. On the drop/hook, slam everything back in at once, full-band and loud — this contrast (near-silence to full arrangement) IS the euphoria, more than any single sound in it.
7. Keep a felt sub underneath the whole hook, tuned to the chord roots, mixed loud and mono — low-frequency energy in the 50–100 Hz "groove" range measurably increases the urge to move, separate from how loud it sounds.
8. Drum feel: aim for MEDIUM syncopation on the hats and percussion — not dead-on-grid (too robotic) and not overloaded with off-beat hits (too confusing) — the medium-syncopation sweet spot is what maximizes the urge to move.
9. Master loud: MClass Maximizer catching 2–3 dB, MClass Stereo Imager widened on the hook only — loudness itself reads as joy and power here, on top of everything else already stacked toward euphoric.

## Why this works
Research found mode moves feeling more than any other single cue, and major (Ionian) sets
the happy floor here; tempo is the next-strongest lever, and the fast 128 BPM pushes arousal
into excited/euphoric territory. Register, dynamics, articulation, and timbre all stack the
same direction (high, loud, staccato, bright) rather than fighting each other, which research
shows makes the emotion recognition especially strong and reliable. On top of that, the
drop-out-before-the-hook move works because contrast — not raw loudness — is what a sudden
return to full arrangement reads as "impact," and medium syncopation is documented as the
exact point that maximizes pleasure and the urge to move (too little is boring, too much is
confusing).

## Order of operations
Chords high and staccato first → additive layers building underneath → felt sub added once
the groove needs weight → drop-out and final loud slam applied last, as the arrangement's
big contrast moment.

## Reason-specific trick
Build the drop-out as its own separate Kong/Redrum pattern (a near-empty one) rather than
just muting channels live — a dedicated "empty" pattern switches cleanly and can be
triggered exactly on the beat. Save the full rack as "Euphoric Build-Drop.cmb."

## RE upgrade path
A dedicated riser/impact FX RE (built for exactly this build-into-drop moment) will give a
cleaner, more controllable sweep than manually automating The Echo's feedback.

## Technique you just learned
The drop-out: strip your arrangement to almost nothing for one bar right before the payoff,
then slam everything back in — the CONTRAST between empty and full is what reads as
euphoria, more than adding another layer ever could.

## Reference
EDM build and drop-out arrangement technique: https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=edm+build+drop+arrangement+tension+release+technique

# Feels: Melancholy

**Accuracy: B** — the minor-third-as-sad-speech finding, the appoggiatura "ache" mechanism,
and the lament bass are all documented (psychology research + music-theory writeups);
combined here into one Reason build.

## The Chain
NN-XT (solo exposed piano or string patch) → SubTractor (lament bass, walking down) → RV7000 (hall/cathedral, long) → MClass Compressor (light, on the solo voice only)

## Steps
1. Set the tempo to 68 BPM — slow, low arousal, the calm-but-negative quadrant.
2. On the Launchkey, play only white keys starting from A — Aeolian (natural minor), hip hop's default sad/serious home mode.
3. Play the progression i–VI–III–VII: A minor, F major, C major, G major — all white keys, no black keys needed at all. This is the "sad anthem" loop; it keeps moving forward instead of just sitting in grief, which research ties to sadness people actually enjoy rather than avoid.
4. Write your melody with a falling minor 3rd somewhere in the first phrase — from the 5th down to the b3 (in A minor, that's E down to C). This exact 3-semitone drop is documented as the most reliable acoustic cue for sadness, because it mirrors how a sad human voice naturally sags.
5. Add an appoggiatura: land your melody one step ABOVE the chord tone on a downbeat (say, D over a C major chord), then let it sigh down to the C a beat later. That "lean then release" is the mechanism behind the classic tear-jerker ballad moment.
6. Under the chords, walk your bass down step by step: A, then G, then F, then E — a lament bass. This centuries-old grief formula works because the ear expects the bass to stay put; watching it slowly descend feels like sinking.
7. Choose ONE exposed solo voice for the melody (NN-XT piano, or a breathy solo patch) and keep everything else sparse — a lone instrument with room to "breathe" maximizes the felt sadness far more than a full arrangement.
8. Drum feel: either leave drums out entirely, or keep them barely-there — a soft kick only on beat 1 of every other bar, no hats. Legato articulation (everything connected, nothing staccato) throughout the melody and pad.
9. Drench the solo voice in RV7000 on a hall or cathedral setting, 4+ second decay — a big reverberant space reads as both grandeur and melancholy at once; let the notes ring into each other rather than cutting them dry.

## Why this works
Research found mode moves feeling more than any other single cue, and Aeolian (natural
minor) sets the sad floor; tempo (slow) is the next-strongest lever, followed by register
(the exposed solo sits in a natural, human vocal-like range), dynamics (soft throughout),
articulation (legato, connected), and timbre (warm, not bright) — all stacking the same
direction. On top of that harmonic/production base, the falling minor 3rd and the
appoggiatura are documented as specific melodic triggers that mirror sad human speech
patterns directly, which is why they do more emotional work here than the chords alone.

## Order of operations
Chord loop and lament bass first (the sinking feeling starts in the harmony) → melody with
the falling minor 3rd and appoggiatura → solo voice choice and reverb space applied last, as
the "room" the grief happens in.

## Reason-specific trick
Automate the solo voice's mix-bus reverb send to increase slightly over each 4-bar loop —
a slowly growing sense of space, rather than a fixed amount, makes the melancholy feel like
it's spreading. Save the rack as "Melancholy Solo Rack.cmb."

## RE upgrade path
A dedicated solo strings or piano RE (physically modeled, with real sustain/release
behavior) will sell the "one human voice grieving" quality more convincingly than a
sampled patch.

## Technique you just learned
The falling minor 3rd: write a melody that drops three semitones (say, 5th down to b3) and
it reads as sad on its own, independent of tempo or chords — because it mirrors the exact
pitch drop of a genuinely sad human voice.

## Reference
Adele "Someone Like You" appoggiatura breakdown: https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=someone+like+you+appoggiatura+why+it+makes+you+cry
