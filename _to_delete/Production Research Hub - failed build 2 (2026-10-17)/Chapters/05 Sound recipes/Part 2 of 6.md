# Feels: Menacing (Dark)

**Accuracy: B** — the Phrygian i–bII move is a documented theory pattern used constantly
in drill/trap and metal; the production chain (sub, distortion, half-time) is drawn from
published horror-scoring and trap-mixing writeups, assembled here for Reason.

## The Chain
SubTractor (sine sub, root notes) → Kong (drill/trap kit, half-time programming) → Scream 4 (distortion on drums and sub, driven) → MClass Equalizer (mono low end) → RV7000 (tiny, dark send for the top layer only)

## Steps
1. Set the tempo to 138 BPM — trap range — but program the drums to feel HALF that (halftime): snare hits land on beat 3 only, not 2-and-4. Same speed, but each hit feels twice as heavy.
2. On the Launchkey, play only white keys starting from E — that's Phrygian mode, the "menacing, Spanish/flamenco, dark" mode. The villain sound is the very next white key up from home (F), called the b2.
3. Play the progression i–bII: E minor (E-G-B), then F major (F-A-C), and back. Both chords are all-white-keys — no black keys needed. That half-step-up move (E to F) IS the menace; it's the most efficient dark trick in this whole book.
4. Put the SubTractor sine sub on the root note of whichever chord is playing (E1 or F1) — keep it MONO. This is felt power, not roughness: a clean low pitch around 30–50 Hz that the body feels more than the ear hears.
5. Separately, add roughness for threat: layer two SubTractor voices a minor 2nd apart (E and F, both mid-register, not the sub) with a slow LFO detuning one by ±10–20 cents. That beating/clashing texture (in the 30–80 Hz modulation-rate sense, not the sub-pitch sense) is what human alarm and scream sounds use — it's a different mechanism from the sub-bass, and you want both.
6. On Kong, program the hi-hats using the tresillo feel (long-long-short, hits on 1, the "and" of 2, and 4) at 16th subdivisions, then add a 32nd-note hat roll every 4th bar for tension.
7. Drum feel: keep swing near 0% (dead on grid) for the drums — rigid and mechanical reads colder and more menacing than a human swung pocket.
8. Drive the sub and drum bus through Scream 4, Damage type Distortion, amount 30–40 — this is the "danger" grit, distinct from the clean sub underneath it.
9. Add one dry, dark verb (RV7000, small room, highs rolled off) on a top-layer texture only — keep the low end mono and dry so it stays felt, not smeared.

## Why this works
Research found mode moves feeling more than any other single cue — the Phrygian b2 is
literally built to sound "wrong" in a way ears read as threat, more than tempo, register,
dynamics, articulation, or timbre could alone. Separately, research on auditory roughness
(clashing tones beating in roughly the 30–80 Hz modulation range) shows this exact texture
triggers fast defensive reactions in the brain because it overlaps with human alarm/scream
signals — that's the mid-register clash in step 5. That is NOT the same thing as the clean
30–50 Hz sub-bass pitch in step 4, which is felt power/weight, not roughness — use both, but
know they're doing different jobs.

## Order of operations
Sub root first (the felt floor) → Phrygian chord move on top → roughness layer → drums
programmed half-time last, so the groove locks onto a floor that's already menacing.

## Reason-specific trick
Route the sub and the distorted roughness layer to separate Scream 4 units — if you crush
them together, the sub loses its clean low-end weight. Save this as "Menacing Phrygian
Rack.cmb" once it's dialed in.

## RE upgrade path
A dedicated saturation/distortion RE with a "warmth vs. grit" blend control gives finer
control over how much roughness bleeds into the sub than Scream 4 alone — worth trying if
you own one.

## Technique you just learned
The i–bII move: step your home chord up to the very next chord in the scale (no black keys,
no dissonant clash needed) and it reads as menace on its own — because it's exactly one
half-step from "home" and never resolves back down.

## Reference
Metro Boomin dark trap loop breakdown: https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=metro+boomin+dark+trap+phrygian+loop+breakdown

# Feels: Nostalgic

**Accuracy: B** — the borrowed-iv trick and the tape/vinyl production chain are both
documented techniques (music theory sources + lo-fi production writeups), adapted here
into one Reason build, not lifted from a single song's session notes.

## The Chain
SubTractor (Rhodes-ish chord pad, I–IV–iv–I) → NN-XT (pitched-up vocal chop, chipmunk-soul lead) → Scream 4 (tape saturation on the whole mix bus) → MClass Equalizer (high shelf cut) → RV7000 (small room, short and dry)

## Steps
1. Set the tempo to 82 BPM — slow enough to feel like looking backward, not dancing.
2. On the Launchkey, play only white keys starting from C — that's C major (Ionian), the "plain happy" mode memory needs as its home base.
3. Play the progression I–IV–iv–I: C major, then F major, then F MINOR, then back to C. F minor is just the F chord with its middle key (A) slid down one key to Ab (the black key just left of A) — that one slide is the whole trick.
4. Hold each chord two full bars. This is a "borrowed chord" — for one bar you dip into the minor/sad world, then come home. That dip is what reads as "remembering something," not "being sad."
5. Chop a vocal sample (or hum one into NN-XT) and pitch it up 5–7 semitones — this is the "chipmunk soul" move: it keeps the human vocal grain but makes it sound like a memory of a voice, not the voice itself.
6. Drum feel: program Kong or Redrum with swing set to 55% (in the 53–57% range) — the classic MPC head-nod. Dead-on-grid (0% swing) kills the nostalgia instantly; it reads as modern and cold.
7. Print the whole mix bus through Scream 4, Damage type "Tape," Damage amount 20–30 — this adds hiss and softens transients like an old cassette.
8. On MClass Equalizer, cut a high shelf –6 dB starting around 8 kHz — this is the "old media" cue: real vinyl and tape can't hold onto the top end, so removing it now is removing "todayness."
9. Send everything through RV7000 on a small, dry room setting (low mix, short decay) — just enough space to feel like a room, not a special effect.

## Why this works
Research found mode moves feeling more than any other single cue, with tempo, register,
dynamics, articulation, and timbre trailing behind in that order — so the I–IV–iv–I
progression (mode swap for one chord) is doing most of the emotional work here, and the
slow tempo reinforces it. Separately, production research on lo-fi and sample-based music
found that vinyl crackle, tape hiss, and rolled-off highs work because they mimic the
sound of childhood media playback — "imperfection by design" reads as human and remembered,
not broken.

## Order of operations
Chords first (the borrowed iv IS the emotion) → vocal chop on top → drums last, swung in
after the harmony is set, so the groove serves the memory instead of the other way around.

## Reason-specific trick
Don't use a "lo-fi" Rack Extension for the crackle — Scream 4's Tape damage type plus a
gentle MClass high-shelf cut gets you 90% of the effect with stock gear, and it's more
controllable. Save this whole rack as "Nostalgic Tape Bus.cmb" so you can drop it on any
future beat that needs the same warmth.

## RE upgrade path
A dedicated vinyl-noise or tape-emulation RE (anything modeling wow/flutter) will add
pitch wobble Scream 4 can't — a nice-to-have, not required.

## Technique you just learned
The borrowed chord: swap ONE chord from major to minor (or back) for just one bar, and
the ear reads it as a memory surfacing, not a mood swing — because everything else about
the progression stayed the same.

## Reference
J Dilla — "Time: The Donut of the Heart": https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=J+Dilla+Time+The+Donut+of+the+Heart

# Feels: Tense

**Accuracy: B** — tone clusters, odd meter, and detuned drones are documented horror/
tension-scoring devices from film-music writeups; the borrowed-V "knife-twist" progression is
a documented theory move. Combined here for one Reason build.

## The Chain
SubTractor (low drone + minor 2nd cluster) → Malström (detuned lead, ±30–50 cents) → The Echo (dotted, creeping feedback) → Kong (7/8 hat loop vs. 4/4 kick) → MClass Equalizer (nothing bright — keep it dark and low)

## Steps
1. Set the tempo to 96 BPM, but don't let it feel steady — this recipe gets its unease from the RHYTHM, not raw speed.
2. On the Launchkey, play only white keys starting from A — Aeolian (natural minor), the plain-sad-and-serious home mode.
3. Play the progression i–iv–VI–V: A minor, D minor, F major, then E MAJOR (not E minor — you need the black key G#, one key left of A). That borrowed major V is the "knife-twist": it's dramatic and unresolved where a plain E minor would just sound sad.
4. Add a low drone underneath: hold a single low A (A1) on SubTractor the whole time, and stack one more note a half-step away (Bb) very quietly — that minor-2nd cluster is what horror scoring calls a "drone of dread." It never resolves, so the tension never releases.
5. On Malström, take a lead or pad patch and detune it −30 to −50 cents relative to a dry copy, both panned mono (not chorused wide) — the resulting slow, seasick beating is a documented "wrongness" cue.
6. Program Kong with a 4/4 kick pattern, but layer a hi-hat loop that repeats every 7/8 instead of every 4/4 — the hats and kick drift in and out of alignment. Irregular meter reads as unbalanced and abrupt, which is exactly the point.
7. Drum feel: keep the hats quantized dead-on-grid (0% swing) inside their own 7/8 loop — a rigid, mechanical hat pattern clashing against a human 4/4 kick is more unsettling than either alone.
8. Add The Echo on the drone or lead, dotted-8th timing, feedback around 40%, no filtering — let it build a creeping, never-quite-repeating wash underneath everything.
9. Keep dynamics low and mostly flat for 90% of the loop, then let ONE moment go suddenly loud (a stab or hit) — the contrast after all that quiet unease is what makes it land.

## Why this works
Research found mode moves feeling more than any other single cue, and the borrowed major V
here creates dissonant expectation-breaking on top of an already-serious minor mode.
Register, dynamics, articulation, and timbre all contribute at a smaller scale — the
detuned, clustered, low timbre is doing real work — but the biggest additional lever in this
recipe is rhythmic irregularity (the 7/8-vs-4/4 clash), which research on horror scoring
ties directly to a felt sense of imbalance, plus roughness from the close minor-2nd cluster,
which taps the same acoustic territory as human alarm signals.

## Order of operations
Drone and cluster first (the tension floor) → borrowed-V chord move → detuned lead →
irregular hat loop layered against the kick last, so the meter clash sits on top of an
already-tense harmonic bed.

## Reason-specific trick
Program the 7/8 hat loop as its own separate pattern in Kong's step sequencer rather than
trying to "feel" it live — the exact, repeating misalignment against the 4/4 kick is what
sells the unease. Save the rack as "Tense Cluster Rack.cmb."

## RE upgrade path
A dedicated granular or spectral-freeze RE can turn the drone into a slowly evolving texture
instead of a static hold — a strong upgrade if you own one.

## Technique you just learned
The tone cluster: hold two or three notes a half-step apart at once, low and quiet, and the
ear literally cannot resolve which pitch is "right" — that unresolved friction is tension
itself, no melody or fast tempo required.

## Reference
Horror score tone clusters and dread ostinatos explained: https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=horror+film+score+tone+cluster+dread+ostinato+explained

# Feels: Triumphant

**Accuracy: B** — the I–V–vi–IV loop is the most-repeated progression in pop music and the
5th-leap-in-brass move is documented across film-scoring writeups on Williams' hero themes;
combined here into one Reason build.

## The Chain
SubTractor (brass-ish patch, sawtooth + low-pass) → Kong (firm, regular kit — no ghost notes) → RV7000 (hall, long) → MClass Maximizer (loud, arena-ready) → MClass Stereo Imager (wide)

## Steps
1. Set the tempo to 128 BPM — fast enough to feel like forward motion and celebration, not a march.
2. On the Launchkey, play only white keys starting from C — Ionian (major), the "happy, plain, commercial" home mode, exactly what triumph needs.
3. Play the progression I–V–vi–IV: C major, G major, A minor, F major — all white keys, all four chords. This is the single most-used "triumph" loop in pop music; it works because it keeps resolving forward instead of settling.
4. On your lead melody patch, play a leap from the root straight up to the 5th (C4 up to G4) at the top of every phrase — that open leap is the exact gesture film composers use for hero themes.
5. Push the SubTractor patch's filter cutoff up and use a bright sawtooth wave — this is a brass-like timbre, and playing it near the top of its comfortable register (not buried low) is what reads as "heroic strain" rather than menace.
6. Drum feel: keep the rhythm firm and regular — kick on 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4, minimal syncopation, swing at 0–10% only. A firm, predictable pulse reads as dignified and stable, which is what triumph needs under it.
7. Play loud — push velocities up across the board (100+) and don't be shy with the Kong pad hits. Loudness itself reads as powerful and joyful here, not just louder.
8. Send the whole mix through RV7000 on a hall setting, 3–5 second decay, 20–30 ms predelay — the sense of a huge space is what turns "loud" into "grand."
9. Finish with MClass Maximizer pushing 2–3 dB of gain reduction and MClass Stereo Imager widened — an arena-scale loud, wide master sells the victory-lap feeling.

## Why this works
Research found mode moves feeling more than any other single cue, and here Ionian (major)
sets the happy floor; tempo is the next-strongest lever, and the fast 128 BPM pushes arousal
up into "excited" territory. Register and dynamics do real work too — the high 5th leap and
the loud, firm delivery are both documented cues for potency/heroism — while articulation
and timbre (bright brass, firm attacks) round it out. Firm, regular rhythm specifically
reads as "dignified and vigorous" rather than agitated, which is why the drums stay simple
here instead of syncopated.

## Order of operations
Chords and the 5th-leap melody first (this is the hero gesture) → drums firm and simple
under it → loudness and hall reverb applied last, as the finishing "arena" polish.

## Reason-specific trick
Resist the urge to add a key change up for the final loop — it's a real, documented trick
(the "truck driver's gear change") but it's also a famous cliché; a firm rhythm plus a loud,
wide master gets you triumph without needing it. Save the rack as "Triumphant Fanfare.cmb."

## RE upgrade path
A dedicated brass-ensemble RE or orchestral sample player will sell the "real horns" version
of this far better than SubTractor's sawtooth — swap it in for a more cinematic result.

## Technique you just learned
The 5th leap: jump your melody from the root straight up to the 5th at the top of a phrase
— that interval alone reads as open, strong, and heroic, independent of tempo or chords.

## Reference
John Williams hero-theme brass analysis: https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=john+williams+hero+theme+fifth+leap+brass+analysis

# Feels: Warm & Soulful

**Accuracy: B** — the Dorian i7–IV7 vamp, chipmunk-soul vocal chopping, and the 53–57%
swing "MPC head-nod" pocket are all documented (music theory + sampling-technique
writeups); combined here into one Reason build.

## The Chain
SubTractor (min7/dom7 Rhodes-ish chords) → NN-XT (pitched-up vocal chop, chipmunk soul) → Malström (low humming pad, breathy) → MClass Equalizer (warm, highs rolled off) → RV7000 (small, dry room)

## Steps
1. Set the tempo to 84 BPM — the boom-bap/neo-soul pocket, relaxed but still moving.
2. On the Launchkey, play only white keys starting from D — that's Dorian mode: "sad but cool with it," a minor mode with one brightened note (a natural, not flattened, 6th) that keeps it from ever sounding heavy.
3. Play the vamp i7–IV7: D minor 7 (D-F-A-C), then G7 (G-B-D-F) — both built entirely from white keys, no black notes needed. This loop deliberately never resolves back to a firm "home" — it just vamps, which is exactly the chill, suspended feeling neo-soul lives in.
4. Take a soul vocal sample (or hum a phrase into NN-XT) and pitch it up 4–7 semitones, then chop it into short fragments mapped across a few keys — this is the chipmunk-soul move: it keeps the vocal's human grain but turns it into a playable instrument.
5. Under everything, add a low humming pad on Malström — a breathy, wordless "ooh" type patch, held low in the mix, barely audible. A wordless voice carries emotional warmth without pulling attention away from the beat.
6. Drum feel: this is THE swing recipe — set your groove to 55% (within the 53–57% "classic MPC head-nod" range). Anything close to 0% swing kills the soul feeling immediately; it needs that laid-back, slightly-behind pocket.
7. On MClass Equalizer, roll the highs off gently starting around 7–9 kHz across the whole mix — a warm, soft top end (not bright/harsh) is the timbre cue for tenderness and comfort.
8. Keep the drums and chords soft-to-moderate in volume (not loud) — softer dynamics read as tenderness and warmth, where loudness would push this toward energetic instead of soulful.
9. Send the vocal chop and pad through RV7000 on a small, dry room setting — just enough space to feel intimate, not grand or distant.

## Why this works
Research found mode moves feeling more than any other single cue, and Dorian sits right at
the edge between sad and hopeful — its one brightened note (relative to plain natural minor)
is what keeps it "cool" instead of heavy. Articulation and dynamics matter more here than in
most recipes in this book: soft, connected (legato-leaning) playing plus the specific
53–57% swing pocket are documented as the difference between "soulful" and either "robotic"
(too little swing) or "sloppy" (too much). Timbre — warm, rolled-off highs, a wordless
humming voice — reinforces tenderness on top of the harmony and groove.

## Order of operations
Dorian vamp first (the chill, unresolved feeling starts in the harmony) → vocal chop and
humming pad layered on top → swing dialed in on the drums → warm EQ roll-off applied last,
as the finishing "soft" polish.

## Reason-specific trick
Dial the swing into ReGroove Mixer rather than manually nudging notes — apply a groove
template at 55% to your recorded hats and chords together so everything drifts the same
laid-back amount. Save the rack as "Warm Soulful Vamp.cmb."

## RE upgrade path
A dedicated tape-emulation or analog-console RE across the mix bus will add subtle harmonic
warmth beyond what MClass Equalizer's high roll-off alone can do.

## Technique you just learned
The never-resolving vamp: take a chord motion that normally wants to resolve (like ii–V) and
just loop it forever instead — the suspended, "we're not going anywhere, and that's fine"
feeling IS the chill.

## Reference
Neo-soul ii7–V7 vamp and chipmunk-soul chopping explained: https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=neo+soul+min7+vamp+chipmunk+soul+chop+explained

# Tony Tone Dusty Boom-Bap Drums — sounds like A$AP Rocky, "Tony Tone"

**Accuracy: C** — built by ear and research, not from the session. This is the opposite of crisp modern trap: dusty, dry, a bit lo-fi, late-80s New York sound. If it sounds old next to modern trap, that's correct. Reason's clean stock samples are the main limit. Real vinyl-sourced breaks would help most.

## The Chain
Kick: Kong → MClass Equalizer → Scream 4 (Tape). Snare: Kong → MClass Equalizer → MClass Compressor → Scream 4 (Tape). Hats: Kong → MClass Equalizer. All three → drum bus group → Scream 4 (Tape) → MClass Compressor → MClass Equalizer → channel Drive.

## Steps
1. Pick drums with body and a bit of age, not crisp electronic ones. Kick: pitch -1 semitone, medium-short decay. Snare: pitch -2 semitones, short-ish decay. Hats: closed, short, level -8 dB so they sit behind.
2. Kick EQ: +2 dB shelf at 70 Hz, -2 dB at 250 Hz, +2 dB at 1.8 kHz, and cut -3 dB shelf at 8 kHz. That last cut is the "dusty" move. Then Scream 4 Tape, Damage 18, P1 30, P2 75, Cut Hi 30.
3. Snare EQ: -4 dB shelf at 100 Hz, +2 dB at 200 Hz (body), +3 dB at 3 kHz, -2 dB shelf at 10 kHz. Compressor 3:1, attack 10 ms, release 150 ms. Scream 4 Tape, Damage 22, P1 35, P2 70, Cut Hi 35.
4. Hats EQ: cut -10 dB shelf at 250 Hz, -2 dB at 4 kHz, -3 dB shelf at 12 kHz. Play hats on 8ths with a few 16th rolls, and leave some bars with none.
5. Drum bus: Scream 4 Tape, Damage 15, P1 40, P2 65, Cut Hi 45. Compressor 3:1, attack 20 ms, release 150 ms, 3–5 dB reduction. EQ +1 dB shelf at 60 Hz, -1 dB at 400 Hz, -2 dB shelf at 10 kHz. Channel Drive 12–18%.
6. The groove (half the sound): quantize at 75–85% strength, not 100%, and nudge the snare 5–15 ticks late. Add shuffle around 55–60% if your sequencer has it. Check the exact control names in your Reason.
7. Optional dust: a vinyl crackle loop in its own NN-XT channel at -25 to -30 dB.

## Why this works
Three moves add up: cut the highs on everything, stack many small tape grit stages, and keep the mids forward. Slower compression lets the drums breathe and swing.

## Order of operations
EQ before the grit on each drum so the grit hears the shaped sound. Bus grit, then compressor, then final EQ.

## Reason-specific trick
Wrap the bus chain in a Combinator, knob 1 = Scream Damage (more or less dust), knob 2 = bus compressor threshold. Save as "Dusty Bus.cmb". Finger the drums on the Launchkey pads, or use the Yamaha DTX400K with sticks, for natural looseness.

## RE upgrade path
None required. Authentic drum breaks from old records would help more than any device. The old notes named other saturation REs. Unverified.

## Technique you just learned
Cut highs instead of boosting, stack gentle grit, and loosen the timing. It works for any 90s East Coast revival or lo-fi hip-hop.

## Reference
A$AP Rocky — "Tony Tone": https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=A%24AP+Rocky+Tony+Tone+official+audio
