# Chords (stacked feelings)

A **chord** is 3 or more notes played together. The Launchkey fingerings
below are all built starting from **C** so you can see the shape — slide the
whole shape left/right to play it from any root note.

## The feelings, with C-based fingerings

- **Major** (C-E-G): happy, plain, resolved. Root, +4 keys, +3 more keys.
- **Minor** (C-Eb-G): sad, serious — hip hop's home base. Root, +3 keys, +4 more keys.
- **Diminished** (C-Eb-Gb): panic, horror-movie sting, "something's wrong." Two stacked minor-3rd gaps (+3, +3).
- **Augmented** (C-E-G#): uneasy, sci-fi, melting feeling. Two stacked major-3rd gaps (+4, +4). Rare — great as a passing "wobble."
- **sus2** (C-D-G): the note that decides happy/sad is swapped out, so it feels open and undecided, like it wants to land somewhere. Root, +2 keys, +5 more.
- **sus4** (C-F-G): same undecided feeling, different flavor. Root, +5 keys, +2 more. Great on pads.
- **maj7** (C-E-G-B): dreamy, cozy, sunset glow. Major chord plus one key below the octave. Lofi/neo-soul staple.
- **min7** (C-Eb-G-Bb): mellow, smoky, reflective — THE lofi chord. Minor chord plus two keys below the octave.
- **dom7** (C-E-G-Bb): bluesy tension that wants to pull somewhere, like a stretched spring. Major chord plus two keys below the octave. All of blues lives in this chord.
- **min9** (C-Eb-G-Bb-D): lush, velvet, neo-soul luxury. min7 plus one extra note two keys above the octave.
- **maj9** (C-E-G-B-D): even dreamier than maj7 — glassy and rich. maj7 plus that same extra note two keys above the octave.
- **add9** (C-E-G-D): fresh sparkle on a plain major chord, no jazzy baggage. Major chord plus one note two keys above the octave.
- **7#9 "Hendrix chord"** (C-E-G-Bb-D#): the happy 3rd and the sad 3rd fighting in one chord — funky snarl and aggression. *Purple Haze*.

## Quick Launchkey cheat sheet

- Want **min7**: play a minor chord, then add the white/black key **2 below**
  the root's next octave up.
- Want **maj7**: play a major chord, then add the key **1 below** the root's
  next octave up.
- Want a **"9"** flavor on anything: add the key **2 above** the root's next
  octave up.

## Use it on the Launchkey

Start every idea on a plain minor or major triad (3 keys), get the mood
right, THEN add the 7th or 9th color note on top once the basic feeling is
locked in — adding flavor before the mood is right just muddies your ear.

# Intervals (two-note feelings)

An **interval** is just the gap between two notes, measured in semitones —
one semitone = one key on your Launchkey, black keys included, the smallest
possible step. Every gap has its own personality, and the classic ear-training
trick is a song that starts with that exact gap so you can sing it to
remember it.

## The feelings, small gap to big gap

- **Minor 2nd** (1 key apart): dread, creeping tension. *Jaws* theme.
- **Major 2nd** (2 keys apart): neutral motion, a simple step. *Happy Birthday* ("hap-py").
- **Minor 3rd** (3 keys apart): the classic sad building block. *Greensleeves*; *Smoke on the Water*.
- **Major 3rd** (4 keys apart): the classic happy building block. *Oh When the Saints*.
- **Perfect 4th** (5 keys apart): open, heroic, fanfare-ish. *Here Comes the Bride*; *Amazing Grace* ("A-maz").
- **Tritone** (6 keys apart): danger, evil, unresolved — nicknamed "the devil's interval." The Simpsons theme ("The Simp-"); *Maria* ("Ma-ri").
- **Perfect 5th** (7 keys apart): strong, hollow, epic. *Star Wars* theme; *Twinkle Twinkle*.
- **Minor 6th** (8 keys apart): bittersweet ache. *The Entertainer* bounce.
- **Major 6th** (9 keys apart): warmth and sweetness. NBC chime jingle; *My Bonnie*.
- **Minor 7th** (10 keys apart): soulful yearning, reaching. Star Trek (original) theme.
- **Major 7th** (11 keys apart): dreamy sting, almost-home-but-not-quite. *Take On Me* (that big opening leap).
- **Octave** (12 keys apart, same note higher): the same note but bigger — a power double. *Somewhere Over the Rainbow* ("Some-where").

## The rule of thumb

Small gaps (minor/major 2nd) = tension or intimacy. Perfect 4th/5th/octave =
strength and confidence. 3rds and 6ths carry the emotion (minor = sad, major
= sweet). The tritone = trouble, always.

## Use it on the Launchkey

Pick any key as your root, then count keys (including black ones) to find an
interval: 3 keys up = minor 3rd (instant sad hook), 4 keys up = major 3rd
(instant happy hook), 7 keys up = perfect 5th (instant power-chord strength).
Play root-then-interval back and forth for a quick 2-note melody that already
has a mood before you add anything else.

# Modes (the 7 moods hiding in your white keys)

A **mode** is the same major scale (all white keys, no black keys) just started
from a different "home" note. Same 7 notes, different note feels like "1" —
and that alone flips the whole mood. Cheapest trick in music: hold down any
white key as home and play only white keys around it.

## The 7 modes — start note, mood, and a song you know

- **Ionian (major)** — start on **C**. Happy, plain, "commercial." *Let It Be* / *Happy Birthday*.
- **Dorian** — start on **D**. Sad but cool with it — hopeful minor, funky, doesn't feel sorry for itself. *Get Lucky* (Daft Punk), *Billie Jean* verse.
- **Phrygian** — start on **E**. Menacing, Spanish/flamenco-dark. The note right above home (one key up) is the villain sound. Loads of drill and trap loops live here.
- **Lydian** — start on **F**. Floating, wondrous, "movie magic" feeling. The Simpsons theme.
- **Mixolydian** — start on **G**. Happy but laid-back and bluesy — "rock & roll major." *Sweet Home Alabama*, *Clocks*.
- **Aeolian (natural minor)** — start on **A**. Plain sad/serious. Hip hop's default setting — most trap beats live here. *Losing My Religion*.
- **Locrian** — start on **B**. Unstable, "something's wrong," rarely used for a whole song. *Army of Me* (Björk) [folklore].

## The extra flavor scales

- **Minor pentatonic** (5 notes, no black keys needed off A: A-C-D-E-G): the sad scale with the risky notes removed — you genuinely cannot hit a wrong note. The rap-melody safety net. *Smells Like Teen Spirit* riff.
- **Major pentatonic** (C-D-E-G-A): the happy version of the same trick. *My Girl* riff.
- **Blues scale**: minor pentatonic plus one extra "blue note" (a flat 5th — one key below the perfect 5th) for grit and attitude. Any blues guitar lick.
- **Harmonic minor**: natural minor but the 7th note gets pushed up one key. Dramatic, "old-world/Arabian," villain-boss-music feeling. *Hit the Road Jack*.
- **Whole tone**: every step is 2 keys apart, no half-steps anywhere. No sense of "home" at all — pure dream-sequence floating. Cartoon fainting-spell sound.

## Use it on the Launchkey

Rest your left hand on any white key as "home," then only play white keys
above and below it — you're now in that mode automatically. Want Phrygian
menace for a drill loop? Hold **E** as home and noodle white keys around it.
Want the hopeful-minor Dorian feel? Same trick starting on **D**.

# Progressions (feelings over time)

A **progression** is a sequence of chords. **Roman numerals** name which step
of the scale each chord is built on — capital letters = major chord, lowercase
= minor chord, a "b" in front = shifted one key flatter (lower). Everything
below is spelled out in **A minor** because A minor is all white keys — easy
to find on the Launchkey. In A minor: i = Am, III = C, iv = Dm, v/V = Em/E,
VI = F, VII = G.

## Grouped by feeling

**Sad, accepting** — i–VI–III–VII → **Am–F–C–G**. The "sad anthem" loop
(*Zombie*, *Numb*). Melancholy that still keeps moving forward.

**Sad, sinking** — i–iv–VI–V → **Am–Dm–F–E**. That last E is a major chord
borrowed from a darker scale — it's the knife-twist of drama in the loop.

**Uplifting** — I–V–vi–IV → in C: **C–G–Am–F**. THE pop "triumph" loop, used
in hundreds of hits. It's the happy cousin of the sad-accepting loop above.

**Nostalgic (borrowed minor)** — IV–iv–I → in C: **F–Fm–C**. That Fm is
borrowed from the minor world for one chord — an instant wistful sigh
(Radiohead/Beatles trick).

**Nostalgic (jazz handshake)** — ii–V–I → in C: **Dm7–G7–Cmaj7**. Smooth
"coming home" motion. Backbone of jazz, soul, and lofi.

**Epic** — i–VII–VI(–VII) → **Am–G–F–G**. A marching descent — trailer
music, stadium-minor energy.

**Dark/menacing** — i–bII → **Am–Bb**. Home chord straight up to the chord
one key above it — pure menace. Drill/horrorcore staple.

**Dreamy** — Imaj7–IVmaj7 on loop → **Cmaj7–Fmaj7**. Two floaty chords
forever = lofi heaven.

**Hip hop / neo-soul (two-chord vamps)** — these never fully resolve, which
is the point: they leave room for the voice.
- i–iv7: **Am–Dm7**
- i–VI: **Am–F**
- ii7–V7 loop (suspended chill, never resolves): **Dm7–G7** forever
- i–v7: **Am–Em7**
- Trap dark-loop favorites: i–VI–III–VII and i–VI–iv–V (Metro Boomin style)

## Use it on the Launchkey

Loop 2 bars of one of these (white keys only, in A minor) before adding any
melody — the progression IS the mood. Rap needs harmony that sits back and
doesn't compete with the vocal, so the two-chord vamps above are your
default; save the 4-chord "epic" and "sad anthem" loops for hooks and choruses.

# Picking a key (and why rap lives in minor)

The **key** of a song is just which note is "home" and whether that home
chord is major or happy-sounding or minor/sad-sounding. Picking a key is
really two decisions: which note, and major or minor.

## Why almost all rap is minor, dorian, or phrygian

Rap's emotional register is usually serious — struggle, menace, cool
detachment — and major keys read as cheerful, which fights against most
rap vocals. Minor is serious by default. Dorian keeps that seriousness but
adds a brighter "cool with it" swagger (its 6th note is raised versus plain
minor). Phrygian adds outright threat (the note just above home is the
villain sound). There's also a practical reason: minor pentatonic melodies
leave maximum space for the voice to sit on top without clashing — the
scale is doing the emotional work so the rapper doesn't have to compete
with it. [folklore, but near-universal folklore among producers]

## The 808 root rule

Whatever note your 808/bass is playing, the ear treats as "what this chord
IS" — the bass is the anchor. Default move: put the 808 on the chord's root
note on the strong beats (1 and 3), and only slide to the 5th or a passing
note between hits, always resolving back to the next root. And always tune
your 808 to the actual key — an out-of-tune 808 quietly ruins a beat that
otherwise sounds fine.

## "Trap keys" — treat this as folklore, not law

You'll see producers repeat that **C# minor** and **F minor** are "the trap
keys." [folklore] Part of that is genuinely because 808 samples often slap
hardest in that register, and part of it is just habit spreading through
stock software presets and phone-keyboard defaults. One informal tally of
Billboard trap hits found roughly 60% landed in F or G (major or minor)
[folklore-adjacent, single source] — but there is no hard rule here. The
real rule: pick the key where YOUR specific 808 sample's sweet spot sits,
usually with roots somewhere between E1 and G1.

## Use it on the Launchkey

Load your 808, hold down single white keys from low A up to about G, and
listen for which one hits hardest and cleanest on your speakers/headphones —
that note is your key's home note. Build the rest of the beat (chords,
melody) in minor, dorian, or phrygian starting from that note.

# Tension and release (the engine of feeling)

Music works like breathing: tighten up, then let go. Almost every trick that
makes a beat feel *good* is really just a controlled way of holding your
breath and then releasing it. Below are the classic moves, plain-English.

## The moves

- **Dominant-to-home pull**: the "V chord" (the chord built on the 5th step,
  E in A minor) is a stretched rubber band; the home chord (i, Am) is letting
  it snap back. The note one key below home (the "leading tone") is the part
  of the stretch your ear feels pulling hardest.
- **Suspension**: hold a note over from the last chord even though the new
  chord has already changed underneath it — it clashes for a beat, then
  drops into place. Feels like a held breath followed by relief.
- **Pedal tone**: keep ONE bass note droning the whole time while the chords
  above it keep changing. Builds pressure like water rising behind a dam;
  finally moving the bass note is opening the floodgates.
- **Lament bass**: the bass walks DOWN one key at a time (A-G-F-E, or every
  single key down A-G#-G-F#-F-E). A centuries-old grief formula — think
  *Hotel California*'s verse, *Hit the Road Jack*.
- **Deceptive cadence (the "rug pull")**: set the listener up expecting to
  land on home (V chord about to resolve to i)... and land somewhere else
  instead (like VI). Stomach-drop feeling — "the story isn't over yet."

## Why it works (the short science version)

Your brain predicts what's coming next in music, the same way it predicts
the next word in a sentence. Emotion shows up in the GAP between what you
expected and what you got — delay the expected chord, deny it, then finally
give it to the listener, and that gap IS the feeling. Sudden loud entrances
after a quiet stripped-down bar, or an unexpected chord you didn't see
coming, are the two most reliable ways to actually give someone chills.

## Use it on the Launchkey

Make bar 4 of your loop the "tension bar": swap in the V chord, hold a
suspended chord (sus4), or just rip the drums out for a beat. When the loop
restarts back on bar 1, it will feel like a release even though nothing
"big" happened — you engineered the breath, not just the notes.

# Arranging separate parts (so two instruments don't play the same thing)

Everything else in this folder is about **one** chord — which notes make it,
what it feels like. This file is about what happens when **two or three**
melodic parts play at the same time. The short version: they should never
play the identical notes on the identical rhythm. That's not two parts,
that's one part doubled, and doubling reads as loud, not as rich.

Real arrangements — a band, an orchestra, a beat with a pad and a lead —
split the work up instead. Five rules, and they all point the same way:
give each part its own job.

## The five rules

- **Different heights.** Two parts in the same octave turn to mush — you
  can't tell them apart even though two different things are happening.
  Spread parts out: one low, one middle, one (if there is one) up top.
- **Split the chord, don't double it.** If the chord is A minor (A-C-E),
  one part can hold the A and E low down while another plays the C up
  high. Together they still spell the full chord — nobody has to play
  all three notes to "complete" it.
- **Take turns.** When one part is busy, the other holds still or rests.
  That's the difference between "two musicians" and "one hand playing
  twice" — real players leave each other room.
- **One is the boss.** At any moment, one part is the tune you're
  following and the other is quietly supporting it. Two parts both
  trying to be the melody at once just sounds cluttered.
- **Silence counts.** A part doesn't have to play every bar. Dropping out
  and coming back in is doing its job, not failing to do it — see
  `tension-and-release.md`'s bar-4 rule, same idea applied to a part
  instead of a whole beat.

## A worked example, in A minor

Take the `sad_accepting` loop from `progressions.md` (i-VI-III-VII: Am -
F - C - G). Two parts, not one stack:

- **Support (low, held):** just the root and the 5th of each chord — A+E,
  F+C, C+G, G+D — sustained, at the chord's own register. This is the
  part that would have been "the whole chord" before; now it's only
  carrying the bottom two notes, so there's room above it.
- **Lead (mid, moving):** the note that's left over — the 3rd or 7th, the
  color tone from `chords.md` — moved up an octave so it's clearly above
  support instead of buried inside it, played as a short figure that moves
  between the support's held notes rather than landing exactly with them.
  This is the part doing the "singing."

A third part, if there is one, sits **above both**, plays even less than
the lead (a passing note here and there, not every bar), and is the first
thing to drop out if the beat feels crowded. Three real parts almost
never all need to play at once — that's rule five again.

## Loops are the exception

A sampled melody loop already IS a finished part — it has its own notes
and its own rhythm baked into the recording. Never add another
instrument on top of a loop trying to "help" it; the loop plays alone,
same as a solo singer doesn't need someone humming under them note for
note. If a loop is playing, that's the one melodic part for that slot.

## Use it here

1 part is normal. 2 is a real arrangement (support + lead). 3 is the
ceiling, and the third should be the sparsest, easiest one to cut. More
than that isn't "richer" — it's the same stack this file exists to move
away from, just with better names on the instruments.
