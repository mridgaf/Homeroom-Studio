# Reference tracks (what the pros A/B against)

A **reference track** is a finished, professionally mixed song you play
back-to-back against your own beat at matched volume — it's the only honest
way to know if your mix is actually competitive or if your speakers are
lying to you. Below are the ones engineers actually cite, grouped by what
to check against them.

## For overall mix balance (vocal vs. beat)

- **Dr. Dre — "Still D.R.E."**: the single most-cited hip hop reference.
  Listen for how the vocal sits perfectly balanced against the beat with
  nothing fighting for space, and how it sounds just as good in mono as
  stereo (a huge deal — if your mix falls apart in mono, it's broken).
- **Mos Def — "Do It Now"**: a textbook example of giving every instrument
  its own frequency lane — kick low, snare in the low-mids, vocal cutting
  through around "presence" range — so nothing overlaps or gets muddy.
- **Kendrick Lamar — "King Kunta" / "HUMBLE." / "All The Stars"**: bass-forward
  but still an even, non-harsh spectrum; the drums sit ABOVE the bass, not
  buried by it.
- **Post Malone — "rockstar"**: two very different vocal takes balanced with
  real space and dynamics instead of both slammed to the same loudness.
- **J. Cole — "KOD"**: clean modern clarity — undistorted bass, punchy but
  controlled transients (the "snap" at the start of each hit).

## For drum and 808 tone

- **50 Cent — "In Da Club"**: unusually tight, controlled bass with drums
  that lead and the 808 supporting — the model "drums first" balance.
- **Jay Rock/Kendrick/Future — "King's Dead"**: several kick patterns
  stacked with zero mud, and a deliberately dirty/growling bass sitting out
  front on purpose.
- **Travis Scott — "SICKO MODE"**: study the glide and tone of the 808
  across its beat switches, and how the low end is saturated (driven warm
  and slightly distorted) to stay big without getting flabby. [folklore —
  a widely repeated producer reference, not a documented engineer credit]
- **Eminem — "Darkness"**: a great lesson in deliberately distorting the
  bass a little so the 808 still reads on tiny laptop speakers, not just
  on a subwoofer.

## The loudness reality (mastering targets)

Commercial rap/trap masters typically land around **-8 to -9 LUFS
integrated** (a measure of average perceived loudness) — noticeably louder
than the **-14 LUFS** that streaming platforms "normalize" everything down
to. [folklore — practitioner consensus, no official published spec] If you
want a safer, still-competitive target that survives streaming turn-down
better, **-12 LUFS integrated with peaks no higher than -1 dBTP** (true
peak, the loudest any single spike is allowed to hit) is a solid working
number. Push louder than that and you start losing punch and dynamics.

## Use it on the Launchkey / in Reason

Drop one reference track on its own channel in Reason, level-match it by
ear to your mix bus, and A/B every few minutes while you work — not just at
the end. Your ears adjust to whatever's loud; the reference keeps you honest.
