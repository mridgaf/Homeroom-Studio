## Part 2 of 11: Tempo

- Slowest: 83 BPM (Break Ya Neck) [UNSURE] [11]
- Fastest: 134 BPM (Forgot About Dre), but it is felt at 67, so it's an outlier [CONFIRMED] [3][7]
- Leaving that one out, the range is 83-104 BPM.
- Most common: 90-95 BPM. Five tracks land there: Still D.R.E. 94, The Next Episode 95, Family Affair 94, Let Me Blow Ya Mind 90, In Da Club about 92 [3][12][13][17]
- Middle of the pocket: 94 BPM.

## Part 3 of 11: Swing

- Swing %: NOT FOUND for any track.
- What IS confirmed: Dre's drum machine had a swing-percentage setting, and he used it. Mike Elizondo says it taught him the space between straight and swung, and that playing straight against swung builds tension [CONFIRMED, Elizondo himself] [18]
- Locked or loose: NOT FOUND as a number. Scott Storch credits part of Still D.R.E.'s appeal to its "sloppiness" [CONFIRMED, Storch himself] [19]

## Part 4 of 11: Drums

16-step grid patterns: NOT FOUND. No transcription or tutorial for any of these tracks turned up.

- **Drum machine:** Akai MPC3000. Engineer Veto: "The brain of the whole thing is the MIDI sequencer" [UNSURE, quoted on a forum] [20]. Dre said he owned at least five [UNSURE, magazine retelling a 2004 interview] [21]
- **Who programs:** Dre programmed the kick and snare on Still D.R.E. (Storch) [19]. Wikipedia says Mel-Man added that song's drums and Dre adjusted them [2]. The sources disagree [UNSURE]
- **Kick and snare sound:** "booming drum-sounds" (Stereogum, Family Affair) [12]. Very high compression (8:1 to 10:1) makes clicky hits like the snare sound thicker and fuller [CONFIRMED, Dre's own words plus analysis] [22]
- **Hot to tape:** the 2001 kicks and snares were recorded loud onto analog tape, so they're slightly crunchy [UNSURE, one magazine tip] [23]
- **Claps:** In Da Club has "sparse hand claps" [16]. DJ Quik: "I gave him those claps, and that kick" [CONFIRMED, Quik himself] [24]
- **Snare reverb:** on The Watcher the snare's reverb tail is audible because nothing else covers it [UNSURE, one forum post] [25]
- **Tambourine and cowbell:** NOT FOUND for this era. Percussion players Jason Hann and Taku Hirano are credited on 2001, but no instrument is named [1]
- **Same drums, many beats:** In Da Club was one of four or five beats built from the same drum programming [CONFIRMED] [16][24]

## Part 5 of 11: Melody and chord instruments

Ranked by how many of these tracks have a source naming them:

1. **Piano or keyboard riff.** Still D.R.E. (two-bar staccato riff, high register, Storch), Family Affair ("staccato pianos that Dre loved at the time," Camara Kambon), The Real Slim Shady (keyboards, Tommy Coster). Job: the main hook, repeating all song. Played live [CONFIRMED] [2][12][22][8][26]
2. **Strings.** Still D.R.E. (high strings come in and out about every 8 bars), Family Affair ("churning strings"), In Da Club ("sparse orchestral samples"), the whole album ("ominous strings"). Job: tension and color, not the hook [CONFIRMED] [22][12][16][1]. Often from a Korg Triton [UNSURE, forum] [20]
3. **Synth.** Still D.R.E. (a synth pluck in the chorus only), In Da Club (synths added late), The Real Slim Shady ("boinging electro funk beat"). Job: marks the chorus, or adds bounce [CONFIRMED] [22][16][8]
4. **Guitar.** In Da Club (an eight-note guitar part, Elizondo), The Next Episode (Sean Cruse). Job: a short repeating figure [CONFIRMED] [16][6]
5. **Horns and organ.** In Da Club only: "blaring horns, funky organs" [UNSURE, one review] [16]

- **Live or sampled:** mostly live. Every Dre track starts "behind a drum machine in a room full of trusted musicians" (Time) [CONFIRMED] [1][18]. Some are sampled or replayed: The Next Episode replays "The Edge" (David McCallum), Forgot About Dre samples No Doubt, What's the Difference samples Charles Aznavour [CONFIRMED] [1][6]
- **Gear in the room:** Korg Triton (on "most of his productions from 2001 onwards" [21]), Nord Lead, Korg MS2000, Alesis Andromeda A6, Rhodes, Wurlitzer, Moog [UNSURE, Veto via forum] [20]
- **Minimoog:** tied to The Chronic (1992-93), NOT this era [21]

## Part 6 of 11: Key and chords

- **Always minor.** Every track with a key found is minor [CONFIRMED] [3][12][13]
- **Loops are 1-2 chords, repeating all song:**
  - Still D.R.E.: Am to Em through an Esus4, short staccato hits [CONFIRMED, two sources] [4][5]
  - Family Affair: C#m to G#m7 (iv to i7) [CONFIRMED] [12]
  - Let Me Blow Ya Mind: i to iv, or i to i(add6) [CONFIRMED] [14]
- **Riff length:** two bars (Still D.R.E.) [CONFIRMED] [22]
- No source shows a dorian major-IV move in this era. That move belongs to the G-funk era (see Part 11).

## Part 7 of 11: Bass

- **What plays it:** live electric bass. Mike Elizondo usually played a Sadowsky 5-string or a vintage 4-string [CONFIRMED, Elizondo himself] [18]. Other bassists on 2001: Colin Wolfe, Mel-Man, Preston Crump [1]
- **Still D.R.E.:** bass doubled with a cello, running all song [UNSURE, one analysis] [22]
- **How it's played:**
  - Melodic and catchy, and it has to survive repetition: "something you won't get tired of hearing for four minutes" [CONFIRMED, Elizondo] [18]
  - It follows the kick, or answers it on purpose, to bring out the whole kit [CONFIRMED] [18]
  - Where a note stops matters as much as where it starts [CONFIRMED] [18]
  - Dre often sang the bass note he wanted [CONFIRMED] [18]
- 808: NOT FOUND for this era.
- Sound: "gut-punching bass lines" (Entertainment Weekly) [1]. Still D.R.E. has about 6 dB less low end than modern rap ("God's Plan"), but punchier [UNSURE, one analysis] [22]

## Part 8 of 11: Mix and arrangement

- **Sounds at once:** Still D.R.E. has 6 instrument parts at its fullest: kick, snare, bass/cello, piano, high strings, chorus synth pluck (counted from [22]) [UNSURE]
- **What runs all song:** drums, bass and the piano riff never stop. Strings come in and out about every 8 bars. The synth pluck is chorus only [CONFIRMED for Still D.R.E.] [22]
- **Silence:** "lean and immaculate... strategic silences" (New York Times) [1]
- **Less is more:** Elizondo: "We didn't want to layer it with too much stuff," yet make it "as big as possible" [CONFIRMED] [16][24]
- **Center, mono:** kick, bass, piano, snare and lead vocal dead center. Only chorus vocals and the synth pluck are wide [CONFIRMED for Still D.R.E.] [22]
- **Compression:** SSL console compressors, ratio "up to about eight or 10" (Dre, Studio Sound, 2001) [CONFIRMED, Dre himself] [22]
- **Steady level:** Still D.R.E.'s loudness range is 1.8 LU, very flat. Lose Yourself is 12.1 [CONFIRMED, measured] [22]
- **Reverb:** NOT FOUND as a rule. One forum post hears a snare tail on The Watcher [25]
- **Saturation:** drums hot to tape, slight crunch [UNSURE] [23]. Little outboard gear used [UNSURE, Veto] [20]
- **Bass ducking under the kick:** NOT FOUND.

## Part 9 of 11: What people who worked with him say

- **Mike Elizondo (bass, keys, guitar, co-producer):**
  - Dre starts a beat, the players add a sound or hook, and it becomes a "skeleton track" [18]
  - About 20 minutes per idea, saved to DAT, finished later in Pro Tools [18]
  - Dre wanted live players so nobody else could sample the same records [18]
- **Scott Storch (keys):**
  - "just throwing shit against the wall and seeing what would work" [19]
  - On Still D.R.E., Storch played piano over Dre's drums and Dre said "That's it" [19]
  - "you have to find the magic" [19]
  - "Dre utilized me as the nitrous oxide" [1]
- **Time magazine (Josh Tyrangiel):** when Dre "hears something he likes, he isolates the player" and refines the sound [1]
- **Eminem:** Dre "wasn't an easy person to please"; on vocals, Dre said "Let's just punch" (record in pieces) [27]
- **DJ Quik (In Da Club):** "making clap sounds that nobody else was doing" [24]
- **Snoop Dogg:** NOT FOUND (no production quote found)
- **Mel-Man:** NOT FOUND (no quote found)
- **Critics:** "Chilly keyboard motifs gliding across gut-punching bass lines" (EW) [1]; "less reliant on samples, more built around clean lines and slick textures" (Stereogum) [26]

## Part 11 of 11: What this means for the current Doc Day, plus a draft description

How the research compares to Doc Day's current settings. Nothing has been changed yet.

| Setting | Doc Day now | Research says |
|---|---|---|
| Tempo | 90-96, 93 | Matches (90-95, center 94) |
| Swing | 50 (straight) | No number found. Keep, or audition a little swing |
| Key | dorian / mixolydian, G-funk chord moves | Plain minor, 1-2 chord loops. Dorian looks like G-funk carryover |
| Chord sounds | 11 sounds at equal odds (choir = bells = wood = piano) | Piano first, strings second, synth/guitar as a chorus-only color |
| Percussion | tambourine, shaker, cowbell | Not found for this era. Sparse claps found instead |
| Reverb | dry | Matches (nothing says otherwise, except one forum post) |
| Drive / sidechain | not researched | Still not found |

**Draft description (one block):** Confirmed facts, except the two gear names (MPC3000, Triton), which are UNSURE.

Dr. Dre's 2001 era (1999-2003), not the G-funk Chronic one: 90-95 BPM centered on 94, always a minor key, built on a one- or two-chord loop that never changes. An Akai MPC3000 holds a hard, booming kick and snare, squeezed by SSL compression at 8-10:1 so every hit sounds thick, running start to finish with sparse hand claps instead of busy percussion. A live electric bass (Sadowsky 5-string) plays a short, catchy, melodic line that locks to the kick or answers it, simple enough to loop for four minutes. On top sits a two-bar staccato piano riff in a high register, strings (often a Korg Triton) that come and go every 8 bars, and one extra color saved for the chorus: a synth pluck or a short eight-note guitar figure. Six parts at most; kick, bass, piano, snare and vocal dead center in mono; loudness flat from first bar to last; clean, with strategic silences. Less is more, but it has to sound as big as possible.
